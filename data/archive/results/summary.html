Student research project · Weather-satellite hazard warnings for trekkers in India

Can a portable weather-satellite receiver warn trekkers of storms?

We ran the SummitSathi alert system on 11,096 real satellite–weather matches: archived NOAA-15/18/19 APT images received by ground stations in India (2018–2025), each compared with what the nearest airport actually reported. Every result below comes from years the model never saw during training.

Question

SummitSathi receives weather-satellite images on a portable radio and turns them into storm alerts for people on mountain trails. The original version was tuned on simulated images. Does it work on real satellite data, and does the satellite tell a trekker anything beyond the usual weather for that place and season?

Method

  1. Real images. 24 SatNOGS volunteer ground stations around India were searched; 14 recorded real images and the rest recorded only noise and were skipped automatically. From their 1,425 archived APT passes, 677 were clean enough to use, giving 11,096 location samples from 13 stations.
  2. Calibration. Each image line was aligned on its sync pulse, noisy lines removed, and the telemetry wedges used to convert pixels to cloud-top temperature (11 µm) and, at night, the 3.7 µm fog channel.
  3. Georeferencing. Each pixel was placed using the satellite's orbit (TLE) and checked against real coastlines. This check found two errors that were then fixed: pixel spacing (India appeared 40% too wide) and an 8-second timing offset (6 clear coastline matches).
  4. Features. Around each of 44 airports: cloud-top temperature, storm structure (coldest top within 50/100 km, overshooting tops, distance to the nearest storm cloud), visible brightness by day, a night fog signal, and cloud-top cooling between back-to-back passes.
  5. Ground truth. Airport METAR reports within one hour of the pass (thunderstorm, rain, fog) and over the following three hours.
  6. Model and fair test. Gradient-boosted trees, tuned on 2020 only. Every other year was then predicted by a model trained without that year (leave-one-year-out). The baseline is the airport's own climate history for that month and time of day, also built without the test year.

AUC: 0.5 is a coin toss and 1.0 is perfect. "Caught" and "Alerts correct" use the hiker-safety alert setting, which is tuned to miss as few hazards as possible, so it accepts more false alarms.

Results: Climb Risk Score (0–100)

The score is the calibrated chance, out of 100, that a thunderstorm, rain within three hours, or fog is reported at the location. It was checked on 7,788 predictions from unseen years. AUC 0.88; prediction error 14% lower than using climate history alone (Brier score 0.074 against 0.086).

Safety rules. The score is never shown below High when storm cloud colder than −40 °C is within 20 km (a hazard followed in 54% of 437 test cases) or an overshooting top is within 50 km (56% of 500). Without a usable satellite view the system shows No data, never Safe; a pass more than 3 hours old is marked Stale.

Limitations

  • Clean images are concentrated in 2019–2021. Results for 2022–25 are weaker (thunderstorm AUC 0.85 on 369 cases) because those archived images are noisier.
  • Ground truth comes from airports. A trail can be tens of kilometres from the nearest airport, and mountain airports (Leh, Srinagar, Shimla) have fewer reports.
  • Rain alerts raise many false alarms, because rain at any given moment is rare (3–4% of cases).
  • Fog is predicted almost entirely from climate history; the satellite adds little.
  • One pass every few hours cannot see storms that form between passes, as in the Kochi case.
  • Cloud-top temperatures assume a nominal on-board calibration target temperature and may be off by a few kelvin.
The system improves on climate history alone, but it does not guarantee safety. It is meant to support a trekker's judgement, not replace it.
